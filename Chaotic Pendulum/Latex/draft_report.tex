\section{Theory}

The central characteristic of chaos is that the system does not repeat its past behaviour, not even approximately. Despite their lack of regularity, 
the aperiodicity still follow the deterministic equations derived from Newton's laws.

\subsection{Conditions for chaos}

As mentioned before, Edward Lorenz had come up with the two conditions for chaotic motion. For a system to be chaotic, it must:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item be described by at least three components that are independent dynamical variables
    \item contain a non-linear term in its equations of motions that couples several of the variables
\end{enumerate}

The sensitivity to initial conditions is a consequence of the second condition. The non-linear term grows errors in predictions exponentially in time so 
any small shifts from its original state will cause that state to become essentially unrecognisable in evolution. It is this consequence that will drive 
the motivation for investigation in chaos theory. Another non-trivial consequence of the second condition is that differential equations containing non-linear 
terms are impossible to solve analytically and so solutions for equations of motion will have to be done numerically.

\subsection{Phase space}

To study dynamical systems, often phase spaces are used to describe states and their evolution. The phase space is a mathematical space with orthogonal coordinate 
directions representing each of the variables required to define the state of the system. Commonly, the axis of the phase space will come in position, velocity pairs 
or analogues. For each solution on a phase space diagram, there will be a corresponding phase trajectory. 

There is an extremely non-trivial and significant property 
of these trajectories, being they are non-crossing. Two trajectories, no matter how close, will never cross each other. This is a consequence of determinism, once a 
state of the system is known, its time evolution into the future and in the past are known as well. A state in two trajectories would render the system indeterminate 
and therefore contradict the uniqueness of the determinism on which the motion is formulated upon. 

Phase spaces of conservative (constant energy) systems preserve the areas in the space. All the points in a given area of phase space will move in such a way to preserve 
the area occupied by these same points at some later time. If the system does not conserve energy, the phase area or volume will contract. This is known as a dissipative 
dynamical system and they introduce a mathematical object known as an attractor. The point is defined as the end state, or fully time evolved state, for all finite states 
that describes the system.

In order to simplify complicated phase space diagrams, Poincare sections are used. They are constructed by viewing the phase space diagram stroboscopically in such a way that 
the motion is observed periodically. Therefore, the Poincare section will provide information about the ratio of the strobing and natural frequency of the dynamical motion. If 
the ratio is rational such that the natural frequency $\omega_0$ is equal to $\frac{p}{q}\omega_s$ where $\omega_s$ is the strobing frequency and $p$ and $q$ are integers, then 
there are $q$ points on the diagram. If the ratio is irrational, then no points are repeated on the plot and so a filled in line of the trajectory will appear.

\subsection{Lyapunov Exponents}
The sensitivity to initial conditions can be measured using a Lyapunov exponent. The exponent, $\lambda$, can be calculated using the divergence of two slightly differing 
initial states, $x$ and $x+\epsilon$. After $n$ iterations, their divergence is characterised approximately as \citep{baker}

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon(n) \approx \epsilon e^{\lambda n}.
\end{equation}

The error growth is, aforementioned, characterised as an exponential and the Lyapunov exponent, $\lambda$, describes the average rate of divergence. The average needs to be 
calculated over many initial conditions spread over the trajectory. If $\lambda$ is negative then the slightly separated trajectories will converge and the evolution is not 
chaotic but if $\lambda$ is positive then the evolution is sensitive to initial conditions and is chaotic. To find $\lambda$, we consider the one dimensional map given by 
$x_{n+1}=f(x_n)$. The difference between two initially nearby states after the $n$th step is 

\begin{equation}
    f^n(x+\epsilon) - f^n(x) \approx \epsilon e^{n\lambda}
\end{equation}

We can rearrange for $\lambda$,

\begin{equation*}
    \lambda \approx \frac{1}{n}\log_{e}\left[\frac{f^n(x+\epsilon)-f^n(x)}{\epsilon}\right].
\end{equation*}

For small $\epsilon$, the expression inside the brackets reduces down to the derivative of $f^n$ with respect to $x$, from first principles,

\begin{equation}
    \lambda \approx \frac{1}{n}\log_{e}\left|\frac{df^n}{dx}\right|.
\end{equation}

Here, we take the derivative through using the chain rule for the $n$th iterate and take the limit as $n$ goes to infinity,

\begin{equation*}
    \lambda = \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\log_e\left|f'(x_i)\right|
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Dimensionality}
So far I have only considered calculating Lyapunov exponents in one dimension. For higher-dimensional systems, the process of obtaining these exponents is non-trivial. It may be 
helpful to revisit the idea and motivation behind these exponents. The Lyapunov exponents measure the average rate of divergence between neighbouring trajectories on the attractor.
The divergence is measured as the distance between the trajectories and so can only be expressed for an one dimensional comparison. If we want to measure the divergence of a two 
dimensional trajectory, then we require two Lyapunov exponents, one for each dimension. The exponents then describe the rate of divergence in uniquely defined, orthogonal directions.
Therefore, for $n$-dimensions, we require $n$ Lyapunov exponents.

We can imagine the a small $n$-dimensional sphere centered at some point on the attractor and the surface of which consists of phase points from nearby trajectories. The sphere will 
evolve into an ellipsoid in time with different axes contracting and expanding. The average rates of expansion or contraction are the Lyapunov exponents and for the $i$th axis, the 
corresponding exponent is defined as 

\begin{equation}
    \lambda_i = \lim_{t \rightarrow \infty}\log_{e}\left[\frac{L_i(t)}{L_i(0)}\right]
\end{equation}

where $L_i(t)$ is the radius of the ellipsoid along the $i$th axis. If we consider, in $\mathbb{R}^3$, a small volume in phase space. The volume will change in time as 

\begin{equation}
    V(t) = V_0 e^{(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3)t}.
\end{equation}

Therefore, the rate of change of volume is found by 

\begin{equation}
    \frac{dV}{dt} = \sum_{i=1}^{3}\lambda_i V(t).
\end{equation}

We can combine it with the divergence theorem, or Gauss's theorem to obtain a relationship for the sum of the exponents, that is,

\begin{equation}
    \frac{dV}{dt} = \int \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}dV = \sum_{i=1}^{3}\lambda_i V(t).
\end{equation}

I need to talk a step back here and talk about how to actually obtain $\lambda_i$ because not only would neighbouring points on nearby trajectories involve points along the same dimensional 
axis, we need to consider points off-axis as well and so our one dimensional view of Lyapunov exponents will no longer function here. I first have to define what I mean by dimension. In this 
section, I will be talking about \textit{capacity dimension}. Consider a $d$ dimensional line, square, cube analogue with side length, $L$. We can split this object into $N$ pieces with side 
length, $\epsilon$. For the $n$th split, there will be $(2^d)^n$ objects with side length $\frac{L}{2^n}$. We can trivially obtain the number of objects $N$ as equal to the ratio of $L$ and 
$\epsilon$ to the $d$th power, that is $N(\epsilon) = \left(\frac{L}{\epsilon}\right)^d$. If we rearrange for $d$ and take $\epsilon$ to go to zero, or equivalently, $n\rightarrow \infty$, we 
obtain the definition for capacity dimension, 
\begin{equation}
    d_C = \lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log_e N(\epsilon)}{\log_e \frac{1}{\epsilon}}.
\end{equation}

The biggest assumption here is that we can take infinite iterations of the set however in practice we are limited, computationally, to a finite set that is a subset of the infinite set (an 
implicit assumption). Experimentally, we can estimate the capacity dimension by using a technique called box counting where a map is covered with boxes of side length $\epsilon$ which is varied 
to obtain multiple values for $N(\epsilon)$. Then the graph of $\log_e N(\epsilon)$ vs $\log_e \frac{1}{\epsilon}$ can be plotted as $\epsilon \rightarrow 0$ and the gradient estimated to be $d_C$.

However, as you may observe from the methodology, this method of defining dimensionality does not account for the changing density in the regions of the boxes, only counting the number of points 
inside the box. There are two other dimension quantities I will introduce. First is the correlation dimension, $d_G$ and the second is the information dimension $d_I$. I will simplify these dimension 
quantities in just suggesting they account for the density of points within the boxes. We can now define a general definition \citep{halsey} of dimensions of order $q$:

\begin{equation}
    d^{(q)}=\frac{1}{q-1}\lim_{\epsilon \rightarrow 0} \left(\frac{\log_e \sum_{i=1}^{n}p_i^q}{\log_e \epsilon}\right),
\end{equation}

where $n$ is the number of phase elements and $p_i$ is the probability that an attractor point falls in the $i$th element. It is known that $d_C$, $d_I$ and $d_G$ correspond to $d^{(0)}$, $d^{(1)}$ 
and $d^{(2)}$ respectively. To learn more about these dimensional quantities, see \citep{farmer}. As we will find, many fractals will require many measurements to characterise their complex geometry.
However, due to limitations of this report, I cannot go into any further detail with these quantities. They represent different methods/approximations in obtaining the dimensions of an object and are 
therefore, inherently, non-equivalent.

\subsection{Attractors}
I briefly talked about attractors in phase spaces, giving a broad definition of an attractor being the end state for a finite amount of states that describes the system. Here 
I will go more into detail about attractors, giving a rigorous mathematical definition. We first should start with the idea of \textit{measure}. In one, two and three dimensions, 
the measure of a solid body $E$ is referred to as the \textit{length}, \textit{area} and \textit{volume} respectively. We are lucky we do not have to deal with any analytical 
treatment of measure, instead we can leave that to the mathematicians and their investigation of the \textit{problem of measure} \citep{tao}. An attracting set, as defined by Milnor
\citep{milnor} is a set $A$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfying two conditions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The \textit{basin of attraction}, $B(A)$, consisting of all points whose orbits converge to $A$, has strictly positive measure.
    \item For any closed proper subset $A'\Subset A$, the set difference $B(A) \backslash B(A')$ also has strictly positive measure.
\end{enumerate}

The first condition states that the basin of attraction must be physically tangible and continuous. The continuous condition comes from the Lebesgue integral definition of measure, 
where measure can be calculated as the area under the curve for some function. Therefore a set of discrete points will produce zero measure. If we are working in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and 
$\mathbb{R}^3$ then our basin of attraction will be defined as an area and volume respectively. The second condition refers to how every point in $A$ will be crucial in creating the 
set. For an attracting set to be considered an attractor, it must satisfy one more condition,

\begin{enumerate}
    \item it must contain a dense orbit.
\end{enumerate}

Dense orbits are orbits that either passes through, or gets infinitely close with, every point in the attracting set. This condition is imposed to avoid describing an attractor as the 
combination of two attracting sets.

There are three nonfractal/nonstrange types of attractors: \textit{fixed point}, \textit{limit cycle} and \textit{torus} attractors. The torus attractor is beyond the scope of this report
and chaotic dynamics in general. 

The term \textit{strange attractor} arises from attractors that exhibit chaotic behaviour, that is, sensitivity to initial conditions. However not all strange attractors are chaotic. The 
idea of \textit{strangeness} comes from the geometric structure of the motion. Strange nonchaotic attractors display fractal geometry in their phase space but lack the extreme sensivity 
to initial conditions, therefore is not classified as chaotic. We will be focusing on chaotic, strange attractors in this report.

\subsection{Fractals}

I have constantly referred to this strange, non-Euclidean geometry as fractals but I am yet to define it. We know that our Lyapunov exponents describe the average rate of divergence in a 
certain direction. Aforementioned, negative exponents correspond to convergence, nonchaotic behaviour whereas positive exponents describe the opposite. Here I will define much more simply, 
Lyapunov exponents describe the 'stretching' or expansion/contraction in different dimensions. For negative exponents, they describe contraction of the orbits whereas positive exponents 
represents expansion of the orbits. Exponents equal to 0 describe zero contraction or expansion.

Kaplan and Yorke \citep{kaplan} developed a heuristic, mathematically imprecise definition for a \textit{Lyapunov dimension}\footnote{This formula acts as a lower found for other methods for 
defining dimension. The relationship between dimension definitions are outside of the scope of this report however it is important to note the varying methods as well as their limitations. Yorke 
and Kaplan's formula is not mathematically rigorous as other dimensionality formulas but it is a very good approximation, hence its use and main reference here.},

\begin{equation}
    d_L = j + \frac{\sum_{i}^{j}\lambda_i}{\left|\lambda_{j+1}\right|}
\end{equation}

where $j$ is the largest integer for which $\sum_{i=1}^{j}\lambda_i > 0$. The condition for strangeness is then reduced to simply $d_L$ must be a non-integer \citep{taylor}. It is important 
to note that for non-area preserving/dissipative systems in two dimensions, chaos cannot arise as a result of the Poincare-Bendixon theorem. Therefore we need at least three Lyapunov exponents 
to observe chaos \citep{Bendixson1901}.

Now we can try to understand fractal geometry. A nice analogy would be to think of a crumpled piece of paper in a box. It is a 2D object in a 3D space. The crumpled paper will try to expand 
(and contract in certain directions) to try to fill the empty space. Of course, our analogy exists in Euclidean space whereas the topic at hand is inherently non-Euclidean but hopefully the 
imagery helps with the understanding of how fractal geometry comes about.

The term \textit{fractals} comes from \textit{fractional dimension}, coined by Benoit Mandelbrot in 1975 \citep{Mandelbrot1975}. In Euclidean dimensions, sets can describe ordinary geometric shapes.
In 0 dimensional space, sets describe points, for 1 dimensional space, sets describe lines (having only length), in 2 dimensions, sets describe surfaces (length and width), and 3 dimensions, sets 
describe volumes (having length, width and height). In fractal dimensions, the dimension index can take non-integer values (as seen with the different ways to describe dimensionality), indicating that 
the set will fill the space. For example, a set with fractal dimension 1.1 will behave like an ordinary line but display some two dimensional thickness. The thickness manifests itself as a self-similarity 
pattern as you zoom into the 'line' more.

\subsection{The Chaotic Pendulum}
I will utilise a simple case study of the driven pendulum to demonstrate the theoretical concepts aforementioned in the previous sections. The equation for the driven pendulum is given by the third order 
differential equation \citep{baker},

\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\theta^2}{dt^2} + \frac{1}{q}\frac{d\theta}{dt} + \sin{\theta} = g\cos{(\omega_D t)},
\end{equation}

where $q$, $g$ and $\omega_D$ are the coefficients of the state variables of the system and different choice of these parameters will produce different trajectories of the system. The state variables are then $\theta$,
$\omega = \dot{\theta}$ and $\phi$ where they represent the position of the pendulum, its velocity and its phase respectively. The equation of motion then clearly satisfies the conditions for chaotic motion, having 3 
state variables and a non-linear term being the trigonometric function in the driving force.

I can demonstrate the usefulness of phase space diagrams in characterising non-chaotic 
versus chaotic by simply tuning the system's parameters as shown in the Figure \ref{fig:chaotic_pendulum_phase_space}.

We can easily observe a drastic difference in the shape of trajectory of motions between two different sets of state parameters. The stability of orbits can be visually defined as a circular path with no beginning or end 
whereas the chaotic, unstable orbits do have a beginning and an end and no clear shape or direction in its trajectory. The path is still circular and loopy but the overall shape is undefined, a intrinsic property of chaotic 
motion. Visually here we can distinguish between periodicity and aperiodicity however in more complicated orbits it may be more beneficial to simplify our phase space. To do this, we will need to define Poincare sections which 
are essentially checkpoints for our trajectories at specific locations. Because of pendulum is driven, we can conveniently choose the strobing frequency to match the driven oscillations. This will produce the diagram shown in
Figure \ref{fig:chaotic_pendulum_poincare_section}.

\subsection{The Circuit}

We need to satisfy the three state variables condition so we need to have 3 integrators and then a non-linear component. We also need to spend an 
op amp to couple the integrators together and so therefore, we can construct the circuit as shown in Figure \ref{fig:circuitdiagram}.

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics[width=8cm]{../Figures/circuit_diagram.png}

    contained two op amps and the input and output of each op amp corresponding to specific pins on the chip. There are a total of 8 pins on the chip.
    Pin 3 and 5 corresponded to the non-inverting input of each op amp whereas pins 2 and 6 corresponded to the inverting input of each. Pins 1 and

    the 2nd op amp of the Chip 1 and both op amps in Chip 2. The final op amp, or the 2nd one in Chip 3 serves as a summing op amp.}
    \label{fig:circuitdiagram}
\end{figure}

We need to apply Kirchhoff rules to obtain our differential equation of motion. If we take the output of pin 7 in the 2nd op amp of Chip 2 as $x$, then 
the 1st op amp of that same chip will be $-\dot{x}$ and the output of pin 7 in the 2nd op amp of Chip 1 will be $\ddot{x}$. Applying Kirchhoff's law of currents 
at the summing op amp output we obtain

\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\ddot{x}}{dt} + \left(\frac{R}{R_v}\right)V_2 + \left(\frac{R}{R_0}\right)V_0 = -\dot{x} + D(x) 
\end{equation}

or more succinctly,

\begin{equation} \label{eqn:equation_of_motion}
    \dddot{x} = -\left(\frac{R}{R_v}\right)V_2 - \left(\frac{R}{R_0}\right)V_0 - \dot{x} + D(x).
\end{equation}

Here I have omitted the $R_1 C_F$ factor we find in the integrator equation by absorbing it into the time derivative, so we are deriving with respect to dimensionless 
time, $\tilde{t} = \frac{1}{R_1 C_F} t$.

\subsection{Numerical Methods (Extension)}
The differential equation \ref{eqn:equation_of_motion} was solved using a 4-stage Runge Kutta (RK4) algorithm. It is a one step, explicit, numerical integration 
method with a run time growth of $O(h^4)$. This serves to be a limiting factor in our analysis of chaos as we must choose an appropriate time step for the algorithm that 
will produce highly resolved trajectory behaviour but also be reasonable in run time. I chose a time step of 0.125 ms for each state solution. 

Furthermore, I employed my own version of Wolf's algorithm \citep{wolf} to calculate all three Lyapunov exponents. Even in this modern day, there is still no stable or 
accurate way to determine zero or negative Lyapunov exponents in time series data. Wolf's algorithm only allows you to obtain all three if you have the independent equations  
of each state. This is because in the algorithm we need to know how each state vector evolves with time. We exploit the useful property of Gram-Schmidt reorthonormalisation
of preserving orientation of the principal axis whilst also providing the subsequent new orientation of the next state vectors, ensuring that the new axes are orthogonal 
(no orientation collapse).

Because we know the equations of motion, we can build the Jacobian for the system and observe how it acts on our principal axis. Since we have three state variables, we have 
to consider three principal axes vectors, 

\begin{equation}
    P(t) = \begin{bmatrix}
        dx(n) \\
        dy(n) \\
        dz(n) \\
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

At each iterative step, the next principal axis orientation will be 

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        dx(n+1) \\
        dy(n+1) \\
        dz(n+1) \\
    \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{J_{n}}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        dx(n) \\
        dy(n) \\
        dz(n) \\
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The Jacobian here contains all the divergence information from previous Jacobians, that is $\mathbf{J_{n}} = \mathbf{J_{n-1}J_{n-2}...J_1}$.
So in each step of the RK4 method, the principal axis vectors are updated with the perturbation from the Jacobian and every couple of steps or so, 
the axis vectors are re-orthonormalised so that orientation collapse is avoided. The stretching factors in each re-orthonormalisation step in each direction are 
recorded and the time average is taken to obtain the Lyapunov exponent in that direction.

I used QR decomposition, which is mathematically equivalent to GSR, to obtain the stretching factors in each direction. Essentially, the new perturbed vector,

The chaotic indicator of 1 positive, 1 zero and 1 negative exponent can clearly be seen in the theoretical plot seen in Figure \ref{fig:lyapunov}. We find that in regions of nonchaos, the exponents behave in quite stable manner but once we get into that chaotic 
region, roughly above $Rv= 65$ k$\Omega$ and below $Rv=105$ k$\Omega$, the Lyapunov exponents start to become chaotic themselves. All three exponents, start to fluctuate in seemingly disorderly. From previous work, we understand that the larger the positive exponent, the 
more chaotic behaviour is observed. For example, in the small region where the largest exponent is approximately zeroed in between the chaotic regions, in the region of $80 < Rv < 85$, we can observe slightly nonchaos. This is better shown in Figure \ref{fig:bicuration_plot}.

The theoretical bifurcation plot agrees quite strongly with the Lyapunov exponent graph. We begin with a stable plot from $Rv=47$ k$\Omega$ to $Rv 53$ k$\Omega$. We then see period doubling until approximately $Rv=68$ k$\Omega$ and then chaos. In regions of 1 zero and 2 negative 
Lyapunov exponents, limit cycle attractors will be observed. These are stable orbits are however then doubled in period, reflected in the two negative exponents slightly converging before entering the state of chaos. Past the chaotic regime, we will observe period halfing and then 
stable orbits again, still with limit cycle attractors. We find this behaviour to still be reflected in the bifuraction plot produced with experimental data, although slightly shifted towards the right. The overall behaviour still remains the same but there are oddities, especially at 
bifurcation points and then near the end of the graph, where the peaks seem to be saturated, capped at 6V. The bifurcation points do somewhat agree, and to be honest, better than expected due to the differences found in previous plots. A further investigation into specific $Rv$ trajectories 
are found in Figure \ref{fig:phase_space}. The values for $Rv$ were chosen based on one nonchaotic, one chaotic, one where the theoretical and experimental disagree and then one post the chaotic regime.

The phase space plots better reflect and visualise the attractors I have been talking about. The strange chaotic attractors are much more obvious to see and distinguish from the limit cycles. Even in the experimental data, we can observe that limit cycles 
greatly restrain the region in which states can exist, creating high density trajectories that produce a circular or loop shape. We can observe that even though in the 2D plots, the loop seems to cross over itself, this does not violate the rule put forth 
by the phase space conventions. We can better see this non-violation in Figure \ref{fig:phase_space3d}.

The loop possesses a 3D depth that is unable to be expressed in a 2D plot but in 3D we can better see this characteristic. The plot of both attractors deceptively show the violation where trajectories cross over each other but rather this is exactly what fractal 
behaviour tends to do. In reality, these trajectories get infinitely close together and weave themselves through one another at an infinitely small scale whereby the pattern is self similar and therefore the trajectories can pass 'through' each other without touching.
We can sort of see this behaviour in a Poincare section where rather than stroboscopically probing the plot at the same frequency or a driven frequency, we observe when the plot crosses a certain plane, seen in Figure \ref{fig:poincare}.

The Poincare plots reveal that chaotic trajectories 'revisit' the same plane but at a slightly different angle/state. This infinitely small change in state is then the fractal behaviour. Finally I want to end the investigation of this behaviour by displaying another 
obvious sign of chaos. Despite the multitude of noise that can arise from circuit behaviour, plotting the oscillations in Fourier space reveals that in nonchaotic regimes, the signal is picked up quite easily and strongly. Therefore, the 'noise' seen in the third plot 
in \ref{fig:fftcompare}, is actually induced wholly by chaotic behaviour, an intrinsic characteristic of the dynamics, not influenced by external factors.

This kind of insight shows that in chaotic systems, the 'noise' that can be observed is largely generated endogenously rather than an amplification of generic noise.

The return maps for the simulated result are shown in Figure \ref{fig:rmap} however experimental curves could not be produced due to insufficient duration of sampling. Overall, the theme of this investigation did not require the fractal behaviour exhibited by these return 
maps, I find that fractal behaviour is better reflected in the plots shown above.

Despite the trials and tribulations in the journey of this report, I still managed to strongly show chaotic behaviour in the circuit built. Although the circuit did not quantitatively reflect the simulated model, plots showing chaotic behaviour could be produced and the 
notion that these regions of chaos existed experimentally were convincinly conveyed. Therefore, the aim of this experiment to validate the findings of Ref \citep{baker} were satisfied and to an acceptable extent. Basic plots for chaotic systems such as phase spaces and 
Poincare sections could be produced for the experimental setup and advanced techniques in analysing chaotic behaviour could be conducted such as producing bifurcation plots and calculating Lyapunov exponents. I found that the chaotic circuit proposed by Ref \citep{baker} 
exhibited near to, but not exactly, 2 dimensional behaviour which agrees with their result and conclusion.
